Two ways to miss a film. Both avoidable.

Either it's sold out — except cinemas hold seats back, and when nobody claims them they quietly go back on sale, often an hour before the show. Or it's not on sale yet — and when booking does open, the good rows go in the first ten minutes, with no announcement anywhere.

Seat Watch runs one watch for each, on BookMyShow and District.

The two watches

Same machine, two different questions.

One of them needs a showtime to exist before it can start. The other exists precisely because it doesn't. Between them they cover the whole of an opening weekend.

And they hand over to each other. When a booking-opens alert is about a single showtime, it carries a Watch these seats button — so the film you were waiting on becomes a seat watch in one tap, and the second half of the evening is covered by the first half of the product.

Watch one · seats

You don't want a seat. You want those seats.

Four free seats in the front corner and four dead centre are the same price and the same row length, and only one of them is worth leaving the house for. So the filters are about size and position, not mere availability — per show, or as a default for all of them.

Friday, 6:40 pm

Your friends land on a Sunday plan. You open the 9:45 IMAX show and every seat worth having is grey. Normally that's the end of it — or you spend the evening refreshing a page in a tab you keep forgetting about.

Instead you click the + on the greyed-out chip and close the tab. At 8:12 pm your laptop buzzes: row F, six together, ₹240. You tap the alert, the seat map is already open, you book normally.

Position comes from the drawn layout rather than from seat numbers, because seat numbers lie: a row numbered 24 down to 01 with no gap can still be two blocks with an aisle between them.

Sold-out shows get the button too

They're the ones worth using it on. And a listing's own "sold out" flag isn't reliable — shows marked sold out have had free seats on the seat map. So availability is read from the layout itself, every time, with no shortcut in front of it.

The popup draws what it found

Pale grey taken, slate blue free, green and banded for the runs that match. Plus a countdown to showtime, a bar filling toward the next check, and a line of free seats over time — which is where you can actually see a release land.

Snooze, or stop, from the alert

Snooze quiets one show for fifteen minutes without unwatching it, and the block it stayed quiet about still counts as new afterwards, so nothing gets silently swallowed.

One tap, and you're where you can act

One show at one cinema opens that seat map. One cinema with several shows opens that cinema's day. More than one cinema opens the film's listing — on whichever site the watch belongs to. You never land on a home page and start navigating.

The seat map isn't a web page

It's a canvas, and the data behind it is encrypted on the wire — so the extension reads the seats the site has actually drawn: where each one sits, whether it's free, what it costs. That's why it can tell six together from six scattered about.

It uses your own tab when you have one

If that seat map is already open in front of you, it reads your tab and no window appears at all. Otherwise a small window does the looking, because the site only draws the seat canvas when its page is visible.

Watch two · booking opens

The show you can't click yet.

The + needs a showtime to exist, and that's exactly the problem on an opening weekend: by the time a first-day show is listed, the good rows are gone. A booking-opens watch attaches to a film instead of a showing, so it can be set weeks earlier — on the upcoming list, where there's nothing to book at all.

Eleven days before release

You're scrolling the upcoming list and there it is — the one you've been waiting on since the teaser. Nothing to book, no showtimes, no date confirmed. There's a small bell on the card. You click it, pick the three cinemas you'd actually drive to, and forget about it.

Eleven days later, at 11:04 on a Tuesday morning, your phone goes: booking is open at AMB Cinemas — Telugu, IMAX. Not "somewhere in Hyderabad". That cinema, that language. You're in the queue before the group chat has found out.

Pick your cinemas — it's worth two minutes

With cinemas chosen, each check asks those cinemas' own listings what's playing on release day. That comparison is exact, and the alert names the one that opened.

Each film keeps its own picks, remembered per city — so watching something in Hyderabad and something else in Mumbai doesn't make the two collide.

Premieres and preview shows

A big film's first shows are often the night before release — benefit shows, paid previews, 1 am screenings — and they're frequently the first thing to go on sale. So the watch covers the night before too, tells you which one opened, and takes you to that day's listing rather than always to release day.

It sleeps until it matters

A film three months out isn't checked at all. The watch wakes a week before release — your call, and it always wakes before its earliest premiere — then checks every ten minutes. A film whose release date couldn't be read is never held back: not knowing when it opens is a reason to start early, not late.

One film, many codes

A film can carry three different listing codes for the same language, and which one goes on sale isn't knowable in advance. The watch matches on the group all of them share, so it can't miss the one that actually opens — and each language gets its own alert as it opens.

Only the language and screen you want

Set a film to alert for Telugu but not the Hindi dub, or for IMAX but not the 2D that opens first. Before a film has listed any screens, it offers kinds of screen instead — Dolby Cinema, EPIQ, HDR by Barco — so the filter works before there's anything to read.

Honest about not knowing

With no cinemas chosen there's no exact check available, only the film's own page — so it reports three states, not two: open, not yet, and can't tell. A detector that quietly read "no" would go silent through the one moment it exists for.

Already-selling films can be watched too: put a bell on something that's partly on sale and the first check quietly latches what's already open, then tells you only about what opens next — the extra language, the extra cinema, the premiere.

Both counters

The same cinema sells the same show on two sites — and doesn't hold back the same seats.

BookMyShow District

A show that reads sold out on one can have a row free on the other. So the same showing is worth watching on both, each watch says which site it belongs to in words, and every alert opens the app it came from. Both watches work on both sites.

Worth knowing about District

District publishes no upcoming list anywhere — no Coming Soon section, nothing under any genre. A film that isn't selling yet appears in no listing on the site at all, and its search box is the only route to the page. So on District the bell lives on the film's own page and you get there by searching for it. That isn't a gap in the extension; it's how the site works, and the demo video shows the search because otherwise you'd never find it.

The bot is yours

You make it with Telegram's @BotFather in about a minute. There's no service in the middle: the token sits in your own browser and the messages go from your Chrome to Telegram. Nobody has to trust us with anything, because we're not there.

Or tell the whole group

A Telegram chat id can be a group as easily as a person, so one machine watches and six friends hear about it — each alert carrying a tappable Book now. Fair warning: everyone is told at the same moment, and on a first-day scramble that means racing each other.

Prefer no bot at all?

A webhook works instead — a Discord channel or an ntfy.sh topic works as-is, and anything else gets plain JSON. Chrome asks permission for that one address, so the extension never holds network access it isn't using.

Desktop alone is fine too

None of this is required. Out of the box it's a desktop notification and a count on the toolbar icon, and that's the whole setup.

When nothing happens

Silence you can explain.

A watcher can't say "I'm not running" while it isn't running. So it says it afterwards: how long nothing ran, how many checks were missed, and whether a show started meanwhile. Ask any time with /status.

The laptop slept

Reported once it's over, in the popup and unprompted in Telegram. This is the commonest reason it looks broken, and it's now the one thing it can prove.

A filter is holding out

If seats are selling but none of them are what you asked for, it says so once — naming what's on sale and what the watch is waiting for.

Nothing has opened

The plainest answer, and the countdown and check bar show it without a word: it's checking, and there's been nothing to report.

Being straight with you

What it will never do.

It doesn't book anything

It doesn't pick seats, hold them, queue for them, or touch your payment details. It watches, and it tells you. You open the link and book normally, as yourself.

Chrome has to be awake

The checks run in your own browser on your own machine. A laptop asleep at 8 pm watches nothing — which is the single most common reason it seems to do nothing, so the settings page tells you how to keep the machine up, and the extension tells you afterwards when it missed a spell.

Seat checks need a visible window

The seat map is only drawn when its page is on screen, so seat checks run in a small window in the corner that you can bury behind everything else but shouldn't minimise. Booking-opens checks have no such requirement — they need no window at all.

It can't create seats

If a show genuinely sells out and nothing is ever released, you'll get no alert, because there was nothing to alert about. What it removes is the refreshing, not the arithmetic of a full house.

It isn't affiliated with either site

Not with BookMyShow, not with District. It reads the pages you're already allowed to read, in the browser you're already signed into, at a pace no tighter than a person clicking refresh.

Privacy

There is no server behind this.

Nobody operates a service here, so there's nowhere for your data to be collected or breached. Your watch list lives in your own browser's storage and uninstalling deletes it.

No account

Nothing to sign up for, nothing to log into, no email asked for.

No browsing history

The tabs permission — the one Chrome describes as "read your browsing history" — is deliberately not requested, and a test keeps it that way.

Your ticketing account is never read

Your name, email, phone and session token sit in the same page data as the showtimes. The code doesn't descend into those branches at all, and a test trips if anything ever touches them.

Open to read

It's a Chrome extension: the code that ships is the code that runs, and you can read all of it. The full privacy policy is one page.

Questions people actually ask

FAQ

Is this legal? Will my account get banned?

It reads pages you're already allowed to read, in your own logged-in browser, and it never books anything. It's closer to you pressing refresh than to a bot: intervals are deliberately relaxed, nothing checks more than once a minute whatever you type in the box, and checks are staggered so they never land in lockstep.

Does it work if my laptop is closed?

No. Nothing runs while the machine is asleep, and evening releases land exactly when a laptop has nodded off. Keep it plugged in and awake — the settings page has the one command for macOS and the one setting for Windows.

Can I run both watches at once?

Yes, and that's the normal way to use it: a few shows on seat watch this weekend, a few films waiting for booking to open next month, across both sites and several cities, each with its own filters and its own pace.

I set a watch and heard nothing. Is it broken?

Open the popup: it will tell you which it is. Nothing ran, because the machine slept — it says so, with how long for. Something is selling but not what you asked for — it says that too, naming what's on sale. Or it's simply checking and there's been nothing to report yet, which the countdown and the check bar show plainly.

How early can I set a booking-opens watch?

As early as the film appears on the upcoming list — months, if you like. It won't check anything until a week before release (or whatever you set), so an early bell costs nothing and can't be forgotten.

Do I need Telegram?

No. Desktop notifications work on their own with no setup at all. Telegram is for alerts that reach you away from the desk, for starting a watch from your phone, and for telling a group of friends at once.

Which browsers?

Chrome, and the Chromium browsers that install from the Chrome Web Store — Edge, Brave, Arc, Opera. Not Safari, not Firefox.

What does it cost?

Nothing. There's no paid tier, no upsell and no ads — there's no server to pay for.
